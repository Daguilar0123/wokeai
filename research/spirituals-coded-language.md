# Spirituals as Coded Language: Research Memo

Compiled 2026-10-18 from web search and fetch. Not peer reviewed. Several anchor texts were reached only through summaries; see Sources for the exact status of each.

Labels: **VERIFIED** (read in a primary or named scholarly text), **LIKELY** (widely cited, named source not read directly), **UNVERIFIED-OR-LEGEND** (no supporting evidence found in what I could read). Where a claim rests on a summarizing fetch rather than my own reading, it says so.

---

## 1. Summary

This memo keeps two claims apart. **(a) Plans and signals:** the strongest first-person testimony is Douglass's account of his own small company in *My Bondage and My Freedom*, Chapter XIX, where a song "had a double meaning" and the North is called the group's Canaan **[VERIFIED]**. Bradford's 1869 *Scenes* records Tubman describing communication by singing **[VERIFIED, via summarized fetch]**, but the song titles many sources attribute to Bradford did not appear in the 1869 text I could read. **(b) Mockery and judgment:** Douglass and Du Bois describe the songs as grief and protest **[VERIFIED]**. I could not locate the mocking lyrics the brief names in any text I could read. Four popular code-song myths have no period evidence for their signal claims **[UNVERIFIED-OR-LEGEND]**. Cone, Levine, Fisher, Lovell and Raboteau were checked only through catalog records and secondary summaries. Thurman, Southern and the WPA narratives were not usefully checked.

## 2. Plans and signals

**Douglass, *My Bondage and My Freedom* (1855), Chapter XIX, "The Run-Away Plot."** Douglass describes a company planning escape that sang a Canaan song, and writes that "the north was our Canaan" **[VERIFIED, Gutenberg text, via summarized fetch]**. The same chapter says a song about lions "was a favorite air, and had a double meaning" **[VERIFIED, same]**. The brief expected this passage in an early chapter; it is in XIX. Scope limit: this is one company, one attempt. Douglass also admits the group did "many silly things" that could draw suspicion **[SECONDARY ONLY, via the Wikipedia summary of Songs of the Underground Railroad; the sentence itself not read]**.

**Bradford, *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* (Auburn: W. J. Moses, 1869).** Read through Gutenberg #57821 via two fetch passes covering the text **[VERIFIED, via summarized fetch; re-check quoted wording against a scan before publication]**. The book says Tubman's communications "were generally made by singing." It describes a hymn that signals danger, one that signals safety, and singing at the Combahee River expedition to draw people onto the gunboats. The last is recruitment, not escape. The Combahee point is a use of song as a signal, but not one of escape routes.

- **Unresolved conflict.** The NPS fact sheet and the Wikipedia summary of *Songs of the Underground Railroad* both say Bradford names "Go Down Moses" and "Bound for the Promised Land" as Tubman's songs. The 1869 text I read does not name those titles. They may appear in the 1886 *Harriet, the Moses of Her People*, which I did not read. **[UNVERIFIED-OR-LEGEND until the 1886 text is checked.]**
- **Sympathy caveat.** A University of Delaware exhibit describes the 1869 book as a mix of Bradford's retellings, letters and reprints, with factual errors and dramatization **[SECONDARY ONLY]**.

**WPA "Born in Slavery" (Library of Congress, 1936–38).** The collection holds roughly 2,300 first-person accounts **[LIKELY, catalog description via search summary]**. I found no narrative in these texts describing songs used as escape signals. This is a real gap in the brief. A 2025 REDEN article says the interviews contain no recorded music but describe the function of work songs **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. Known biases stand: white interviewers, Jim Crow-era conditions, elderly informants.

**Net assessment.** Documented first-person evidence for song-as-signal is thin. It rests on Douglass's single company and on Tubman as reported by a sympathetic biographer. The evidence supports signaling by song in specific cases. It does not establish a general code used across plantations.

## 3. Mockery and judgment

- **Douglass, *Narrative* (1845), Chapter II.** The songs "told a tale of woe" beyond a child's comprehension **[VERIFIED, Gutenberg text]**. This is the grief reading, not ridicule.
- **Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903).** The Forethought calls the songs "the only American music which welled up from black souls in the dark past" **[VERIFIED, Gutenberg text]**. Chapter XIV, "Of the Sorrow Songs," says they are "the music of an unhappy people, of the children of disappointment" **[VERIFIED, Gutenberg text]**. The chapter's framing is sorrow and judgment on the conditions that produced it. I did not read it closely enough to establish mockery of masters.
- **Levine, *Black Culture and Black Consciousness* (1977), Chapter 1, "The Sacred World of Black Slaves."** Contents **[VERIFIED, library catalog record]**. Levine's specific song examples were not verified. One secondary source (a Harvard essay on black humor by Werner Sollors, citing Levine) reports an enslaved man who answers a charge over a dead pig by arguing the master had less pig but more people **[LIKELY; a joke, not a song]**.
- **"Everybody talkin' 'bout heaven ain't goin' there."** Not located in any text I could read. The 1867 *Slave Songs of the United States* is the main early collection, but I could not check its contents. **[UNVERIFIED-OR-LEGEND.]**
- **"Got one mind for the boss to see…"** A University of Illinois catalog entry reads a verse as protective performance **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. I did not confirm its source or date. **[UNVERIFIED.]**
- **"My ole missus promised me…" family.** A folklorist.org entry describes the group: a promise of freedom at the owner's death, with variants that share verses **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. A search snippet pointed to an 1887 dime-novel listing as the earliest print; I did not read it **[UNVERIFIED]**. I could not confirm Levine's use of this song **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **Michigan-Flint thesis (1984).** Argues that work songs and children's songs often mocked owners as passive resistance **[SECONDARY ONLY; thesis not read; UNVERIFIED]**.

## 4. Cone's reading

- **Cone, *The Spirituals and the Blues* (1972; Orbis 1991).** Not read. A 2021 CPR essay describes Cone calling the blues "secular spirituals" and reading the spirituals as coded language about daily life and hope for present and future transcendence **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. It also describes the songs as resistance and endurance **[SECONDARY ONLY]**.
- **Brief's claims not checked.** The claims that heaven and Canaan name this-worldly freedom and the North, and that the songs judge white Christianity, are not verified against Cone's text. Douglass's Chapter XIX passage is the primary source that would support the first claim. Whether Cone cites it I could not check. No page numbers are given because none could be verified.
- **Skeptical counter-reading.** The Wikipedia summary of *Songs of the Underground Railroad* reports that Joel Bresler and James Kelley (2008) read the songs as hope for deliverance from earthly sorrow, not literal help to runaways **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. That distinction is one Cone's reading does not settle.

## 6. Connection to "woke"

Lead Belly's "Scottsboro Boys," recorded in New York in 1938 and now on Document Records' *Remaining Library of Congress Recordings, Vol. 5* (1997), carries a spoken aside: "best stay woke, keep their eyes open" **[LIKELY, quoted in Snopes; I did not listen to the recording]**. One source places the aside on a 2015 Smithsonian Folkways box set **[LIKELY]**. The Current calls it probably the first recorded use of the phrase; D Magazine argues the aside is a spoken remark and that earlier use cannot be ruled out. The connection is one of form, not descent. The spirituals' double meaning, which Douglass describes as a song that "had a double meaning," is an ancestor of a phrase that reads one way to one audience and another way to another. The aside tells listeners to stay alert on a dangerous route, which makes the parallel a matter of structure, not a thesis.
