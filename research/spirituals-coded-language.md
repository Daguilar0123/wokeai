## 1. Summary

This memo keeps two claims apart. **(a) Plans and signals:** the strongest first-person testimony is Douglass's account of his own small company in *My Bondage and My Freedom*, Chapter XIX, where a song "had a double meaning" and the North is called the group's Canaan **[VERIFIED]**. Bradford's two books, now both read directly, record Tubman describing communication by singing **[VERIFIED]**. Both print her farewell song ending "boun' for de promised land"; the 1869 book says its words "meant something more" than a heavenly journey. The 1869 book gives a safe-or-danger pair of songs, with a "Moses go down in Egypt" variant as the danger signal. The 1886 book prints the "Go Down, Moses" lyric as the song she sang to announce herself to hidden fugitives. Neither book uses "Go Down, Moses" or "Bound for the Promised Land" as a title, so the NPS and Wikipedia summaries are right that the songs are there and loose about how they are named. *Slave Songs of the United States* (1867) adds two printed notes, both from Col. Higginson: one song "sung in secret," and one verse read by a listener as meaning the Yankees **[VERIFIED, read directly]**. All of this is Tubman as told to a sympathetic biographer, plus one officer's retelling; it supports signaling by song in specific cases, not a general code. **(b) Mockery and judgment:** Douglass and Du Bois describe the songs as grief and protest **[VERIFIED]**. The mocking lyrics the brief names ("Everybody talkin' 'bout heaven," "one mind for the boss," the "ole missus" family) are **VERIFIED ABSENT** from the 1867 *Slave Songs*, searched in two copies; the claims stay **UNVERIFIED-OR-LEGEND**. That book does print "The Hypocrite and the Concubine" (No. 91), a song that judges religious hypocrisy without saying whose **[VERIFIED]**. Levine could not be reached **[UNVERIFIED]**. Four popular code-song myths have no period evidence for their signal claims **[UNVERIFIED-OR-LEGEND]**. Cone could not be reached; his section stays **[UNVERIFIED]**. Fisher, Lovell and Raboteau were checked only through catalog records and secondary summaries. Thurman, Southern and the WPA narratives were not usefully checked. On "stay woke": two Library of Congress-held recordings of "Scottsboro Boys" are documented (New York, 1938-12-26; Washington, 1940-08-23), the Folkways box set carries the 1940 one, and no primary catalog I could reach mentions the spoken aside **[aside: LIKELY at most]**.

## 2. Plans and signals

**Douglass, *My Bondage and My Freedom* (1855), Chapter XIX, "The Run-Away Plot."** Douglass describes a company planning escape that sang a Canaan song, and writes that "the north was our Canaan" **[VERIFIED, Gutenberg text, via summarized fetch]**. The same chapter says a song about lions "was a favorite air, and had a double meaning" **[VERIFIED, same]**. The brief expected this passage in an early chapter; it is in XIX. Scope limit: this is one company, one attempt. Douglass also admits the group did "many silly things" that could draw suspicion **[SECONDARY ONLY, via the Wikipedia summary of Songs of the Underground Railroad; the sentence itself not read]**.

**Bradford, *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* (Auburn: W. J. Moses, 1869).** Second pass: full text downloaded from Gutenberg #57821 and from DocSouth (the DocSouth edition carries page markers, used for the page numbers here) and searched locally **[VERIFIED, read directly]**. This replaces the first pass's "via summarized fetch." The first-pass reading holds: Tubman's "communications were generally made by singing" (p. 17). The details, with the song titles absent and the lyrics present:

- **Farewell song, pp. 17–19.** Tubman sings, as she leaves, a farewell that repeats "I'm boun' for de promised land" (to her family and then to the master on the road). Bradford comments that the words "meant something more than a journey to the Heavenly Canaan" (p. 17). That is the book's own statement of double meaning.
- **Safe signal and danger signal, pp. 26–27.** A hymn beginning "Hail, oh hail ye happy spirits" tells hidden fugitives their deliverer is near (p. 26). Tubman says if she sang "Moses go down in Egypt" instead, they would not come out, "for dere's danger in de way" (p. 27). Bradford says she gives the words "exactly as Harriet sang them to me" (p. 26). So in 1869 the Moses song, in a variant, is the danger signal. The first pass described the signal pair but did not note that the danger signal is a Moses lyric.
- **Combahee River, p. 41.** Col. Montgomery tells her "Moses, you'll have to give 'em a song," and she sings a patriotic verse about the Union (not a spiritual) to draw people onto the gunboats. The first pass called this recruitment, not escape; confirmed.

**Bradford, *Harriet, the Moses of Her People* (New York: Geo. R. Lockwood & Son, 1886).** Second pass: DocSouth full text downloaded and searched **[VERIFIED, read directly]**. That encoding has page markers and no chapter divisions, so citations are by page. The preface calls this the "second edition," "in part re-written" (pp. 3, 5). Evidence:

- **p. 27:** "their communication was often made by singing," with hymns about the heavenly journey and Canaan conveying "something more than met the ear."
- **p. 28:** the same farewell lyric, "I'm boun' for de promised land."
- **pp. 36–38:** Bradford writes that she has "so often heard them sung by herself" (p. 36), meaning Tubman, who is her source. Tubman approaches hidden fugitives, and "to make them certain that it is their leader who is coming" (p. 37) she sings "the song, forbidden to her people at the South" (p. 37). The lyric printed is "Oh go down, Moses" (pp. 37–38), with the standard words of the well-known song. Here the song announces her; it is not the danger signal of 1869. The 1869 anecdote about not coming out when "Moses go down in Egypt" is sung was not found in the 1886 text by search.

- **Resolved (second pass, 2026-10-18): do Bradford's books name "Go Down Moses" and "Bound for the Promised Land" as Tubman's songs?** In substance yes, for the lyrics; not by title. In both books, the string "Bound for the Promised Land" occurs nowhere (the lyric reads "boun' for de promised land"), and the words "go down, Moses" occur only inside the 1886 lyric, never as a title or heading. The NPS fact sheet and the Wikipedia summary of *Songs of the Underground Railroad* are therefore accurate that the songs appear and loose about naming. The first pass said the 1869 text "does not name those titles." That is true of titles but missed that the 1869 text prints the promised-land lyric and a Moses variant. **[VERIFIED, read directly: lyric of the promised-land farewell in both editions; Moses lyric as a danger signal in 1869; full "Go Down, Moses" lyric in 1886.]** What stays open is only whether any of this reflects 1850s practice, since every version passes through Bradford.
- **Sympathy caveat.** A University of Delaware exhibit describes the 1869 book as a mix of Bradford's retellings, letters and reprints, with factual errors and dramatization **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. The two books also differ on the Moses song's role, which Bradford does not reconcile.

**Higginson's notes in *Slave Songs of the United States* (1867).** Second pass, read directly **[VERIFIED that the editors print these; the claims inside them rest on Col. T. W. Higginson, whom the editors thank on p. xxxvii and who initials these notes "T. W. H."]**. A note on No. 64, "Many Thousand Go" (p. 48), says the song "had been sung in secret to avoid detection." A note on No. 112, "My Father, How Long?" (p. 93), says singers were jailed at Georgetown, S. C., for it at the start of the Civil War because "'We'll soon be free' was too dangerous an assertion," and says the verse about the Lord calling us home "was evidently thought to be a symbolical verse," quoting a drummer boy: "Dey tink de Lord mean for say de Yankees." This is wartime, Sea Islands and Georgetown evidence of double meaning in a song, told by a white officer. It does not show escape routes.

**WPA "Born in Slavery" (Library of Congress, 1936–38).** The collection holds roughly 2,300 first-person accounts **[LIKELY, catalog description via search summary]**. I found no narrative in these texts describing songs used as escape signals. This is a real gap in the brief. A 2025 REDEN article says the interviews contain no recorded music but describe the function of work songs **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. Known biases stand: white interviewers, Jim Crow-era conditions, elderly informants.

**Net assessment.** Documented first-person evidence for song-as-signal is thin. It rests on Douglass's single company, on Tubman as reported by a sympathetic biographer in two differing books, and on Higginson's two printed notes. The evidence supports signaling by song in specific cases. It does not establish a general code used across plantations.

## 3. Mockery and judgment

- **Douglass, *Narrative* (1845), Chapter II.** The songs "told a tale of woe" beyond a child's comprehension **[VERIFIED, Gutenberg text]**. This is the grief reading, not ridicule.
- **Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903).** The Forethought calls the songs "the only American music which welled up from black souls in the dark past" **[VERIFIED, Gutenberg text]**. Chapter XIV, "Of the Sorrow Songs," says they are "the music of an unhappy people, of the children of disappointment" **[VERIFIED, Gutenberg text]**. The chapter's framing is sorrow and judgment on the conditions that produced it. I did not read it closely enough to establish mockery of masters.
- **Levine, *Black Culture and Black Consciousness* (1977), Chapter 1, "The Sacred World of Black Slaves."** Contents **[VERIFIED, library catalog record]**. One secondary source (a Harvard essay on black humor by Werner Sollors, citing Levine) reports an enslaved man who answers a charge over a dead pig by arguing the master had less pig but more people **[LIKELY; a joke, not a song]**. **Second pass: could not be reached; still unverified.** The archive.org scan (id blackcultureblac00levi, Oxford University Press, 1977) is lendable only to a logged-in borrower; the full-text file returned HTTP 401, and I did not create an account or log in. Whether Levine uses the "ole missus" or "one mind for the boss" songs, and what he says about them, is **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **"Everybody talkin' 'bout heaven ain't goin' there."** Second pass: I searched the full text of *Slave Songs of the United States* (1867), in the DocSouth transcription and in an archive.org scan's OCR, for the words and for dialect spellings ("heaven/heben," "talk," "goin'"). **[VERIFIED ABSENT from the 1867 book.]** The editors say they obtained "only a very few songs of this character," meaning secular songs (p. x), and could not form a separate secular section (p. xx), so absence from that book is weak evidence either way. The song claim stays **[UNVERIFIED-OR-LEGEND]**.
- **"Got one mind for the boss to see…"** A University of Illinois catalog entry reads a verse as protective performance **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. I did not confirm its source or date. Second pass: the words ("one mind," "boss to see") are **[VERIFIED ABSENT from the 1867 book]** under the same search. The only "boss" in the book appears in the editors' specimens of ordinary speech (a school scene, p. xxxiii, and a man's talk about love, p. xxxvi), not in any song. **[UNVERIFIED.]**
- **"My ole missus promised me…" family.** A folklorist.org entry describes the group: a promise of freedom at the owner's death, with variants that share verses **[SECONDARY ONLY]**. A search snippet pointed to an 1887 dime-novel listing as the earliest print; I did not read it **[UNVERIFIED]**. Second pass: I searched the 1867 *Slave Songs* for "missus," "mistress," "promise" and "set me free" and found no song of that family **[VERIFIED ABSENT]**. Related items that are present are release songs, not mockery: "Done wid missus' scoldin'" (a variant verse, p. 45) and "No more mistress' call for me" (No. 64, "Many Thousand Go," p. 48) **[VERIFIED, read directly]**. I could not confirm Levine's use of this song **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **"The Hypocrite and the Concubine" (No. 91, p. 70).** Second pass, read directly **[VERIFIED that it is printed, as a Virginia song collected by W. F. A.]**. It says the hypocrite and concubine "run to God with the lips and tongue" and "leave all the heart behind." This is a judgment song about religious hypocrisy. The book does not say whose hypocrisy is meant, so whether it judges white Christianity is **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **Michigan-Flint thesis (1984).** Argues that work songs and children's songs often mocked owners as passive resistance **[SECONDARY ONLY; thesis not read; UNVERIFIED]**.

## 6. Connection to "woke"

Lead Belly's "Scottsboro Boys" is said to carry a spoken aside: "best stay woke, keep their eyes open" **[LIKELY, as quoted in Snopes; I did not listen to the recording, and no primary catalog I reached mentions the aside]**. The first pass wrote that the song was "recorded in New York in 1938" and "now on Document Records' *Remaining Library of Congress Recordings, Vol. 5* (1997)," and also that one source places the aside on a 2015 Smithsonian Folkways box set. Second pass (primary catalogs, 2026-10-18) shows those two statements point at different recordings:

- **1938, New York.** The Library of Congress American Folklife Center's Lead Belly research guide (last updated 2026-01-13 as displayed) describes a session recorded and interviewed by Alan Lomax at Havers Studio, New York City, December 26, 1938, whose performances include "Scottsboro Boys" **[VERIFIED, read directly]**.
- **1940, Washington.** The booklet of the 2015 box set *Lead Belly: The Smithsonian Folkways Collection* (SFW40201) lists "Scottsboro Boys" as track 16 of Disc One (4:32) and gives its source as "Library of Congress disc 4473A3; recorded August 23, 1940, in Washington, D.C." **[VERIFIED, read directly]**. The Folkways track page (SFW40201_116, released 2015, 4:32) gives no recording date **[VERIFIED, read directly]**. The same LOC guide describes an August 23, 1940 session by Lomax in the Coolidge Auditorium, 50 performances, without listing titles **[VERIFIED]**.
- **So two recordings are documented, and the Folkways box set carries the 1940 one,** not the 1938 one. The first pass's pairing of "1938" with the box set was a conflation.
- **The aside.** The booklet's text contains no "woke" in connection with this track, and the LOC guide does not mention it. Which recording carries the aside, if either does, is **[UNVERIFIED]**. Not checked: the audio itself, the Document Records release, and the two LOC catalog-card pages (items 37 below), which sit behind a bot-verification screen I did not pass.

The Current calls it probably the first recorded use of the phrase; D Magazine argues the aside is a spoken remark and that earlier use cannot be ruled out. The connection is one of form, not descent. The spirituals' double meaning, which Douglass describes as a song that "had a double meaning," is an ancestor of a phrase that reads one way to one audience and another way to another. The aside tells listeners to stay alert on a dangerous route, which makes the parallel a matter of structure, not a thesis.
